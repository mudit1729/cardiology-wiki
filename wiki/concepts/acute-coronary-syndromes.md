# Acute Coronary Syndromes

Acute coronary syndromes (ACS) represent a spectrum of acute myocardial ischemia ranging from unstable angina to non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) to ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). The underlying pathophysiology involves acute coronary plaque disruption (rupture, erosion, or calcified nodule) with superimposed thrombosis causing abrupt reduction or cessation of myocardial blood flow. ACS management demands rapid triage, risk stratification, and timely revascularization.

## Key Principles

- **Time is myocardium in STEMI**: Primary PCI ([[pci-basics]]) within 120 minutes of first medical contact is the standard of care. Every 30-minute delay in reperfusion increases mortality. Systems-level optimization (STEMI networks, cath lab activation protocols) is essential
- **Risk stratification in NSTEMI**: GRACE score guides timing of invasive strategy. Very high-risk (hemodynamic instability, ongoing ischemia, arrhythmia) requires immediate catheterization (<2 hours). High-risk features warrant early invasive strategy (<24 hours). Intermediate risk allows selective invasive strategy
- **High-sensitivity troponin revolutionizes diagnosis**: hs-cTn enables rapid rule-in (0/1-hour or 0/2-hour algorithms) and rule-out of acute MI. Understanding the kinetics and assay-specific cutoffs is essential for accurate ACS diagnosis
- **Dual antiplatelet therapy**: Aspirin plus a P2Y12 inhibitor ([[antiplatelet-therapy]]) is mandatory. Potent P2Y12 inhibitors (ticagrelor, prasugrel) preferred over clopidogrel in most ACS patients. Pre-treatment with P2Y12 inhibitors before angiography is debated (avoid prasugrel pre-treatment per ACCOAST)
- **Anticoagulation in ACS**: Unfractionated heparin, enoxaparin, bivalirudin, or fondaparinux based on clinical context and invasive strategy timing. Fondaparinux recommended for conservative strategy. UFH or enoxaparin for invasive strategy
- **Culprit-only PCI in STEMI with shock**: In cardiogenic shock ([[cardiogenic-shock]]), treat only the culprit lesion acutely (CULPRIT-SHOCK trial). Complete revascularization during index hospitalization
- **Complete revascularization in STEMI without shock**: COMPLETE trial supports complete revascularization of non-culprit lesions before discharge or within 45 days. Physiology-guided assessment of non-culprit lesions recommended
- **Plaque mechanism matters**: OCT ([[oct-procedure]]) can differentiate plaque rupture, erosion, and calcified nodule. Plaque erosion may be managed conservatively with antithrombotic therapy alone (EROSION study)
- **Mechanical complications**: Free wall rupture, ventricular septal defect, and papillary muscle rupture are rare but life-threatening MI complications requiring urgent surgical consultation
- **Secondary prevention starts in-hospital**: Initiate high-intensity statin, beta-blocker (if LVEF reduced), ACE inhibitor/ARB, SGLT2 inhibitor (if diabetes or HF), and cardiac rehabilitation referral before discharge

## ACS Classification and Diagnosis

- **STEMI**: ST-elevation in >= 2 contiguous leads (>= 1 mm in limb leads, >= 2 mm in precordial leads) or new LBBB with clinical suspicion. Requires emergent reperfusion
- **NSTEMI**: Rise and/or fall of cardiac troponin (hs-cTn) with at least one value above the 99th percentile URL, plus clinical evidence of ischemia (symptoms, ECG changes, imaging findings)
- **Unstable Angina**: Clinical presentation consistent with ACS but without troponin elevation. Declining diagnosis in the era of high-sensitivity troponin assays
- **Type 2 MI**: Myocardial oxygen supply-demand mismatch without acute atherothrombotic event (tachycardia, anemia, respiratory failure, severe hypertension). Management targets the underlying cause rather than invasive strategy
- **MINOCA**: MI with non-obstructive coronary arteries (<50% stenosis). Requires systematic workup: OCT for plaque disruption, cardiac MRI for myocarditis vs. MI, provocative testing for vasospasm

## Key Trials

- [[PLATO]] - Ticagrelor vs. clopidogrel in ACS: ticagrelor reduced CV death, MI, and stroke
- [[TRITON-TIMI-38]] - Prasugrel vs. clopidogrel in ACS undergoing PCI: prasugrel superior but more bleeding
- [[COMPLETE]] - Complete vs. culprit-only revascularization in STEMI: complete revascularization reduced CV death and MI
- [[CULPRIT-SHOCK]] - Culprit-only vs. multivessel PCI in AMI with cardiogenic shock: culprit-only reduced 30-day death and renal replacement therapy
- [[MATRIX]] - Radial vs. femoral access in ACS: radial reduced net adverse clinical events
- [[ACCOAST]] - Pre-treatment with prasugrel in NSTEMI: increased bleeding without benefit
- [[DUBIUS]] - Early vs. delayed invasive strategy in NSTEMI
- [[ISAR-REACT-5]] - Prasugrel vs. ticagrelor in ACS patients undergoing PCI
- [[EROSION]] - Conservative management of plaque erosion identified by OCT
- [[DANAMI-3-DEFER]] - Deferred vs. immediate stenting in STEMI (no benefit for deferred approach)
- [[TOTAL]] - Routine aspiration thrombectomy in STEMI: no benefit
- [[EARLY-BAMI]] - Routine use of IABP in STEMI without shock: no benefit

## Guideline Recommendations

- **ESC 2023 ACS (NSTE-ACS) Guidelines**: Risk stratification with GRACE score for all NSTEMI patients. Immediate invasive strategy for very high-risk patients. Early invasive (<24 hours) for high-risk features. Ticagrelor or prasugrel preferred over clopidogrel. Radial access first-line. De-escalation of antiplatelet therapy may be considered after 1-3 months in selected patients
- **ESC 2023 STEMI Guidelines**: Primary PCI as default reperfusion strategy. Door-to-balloon target <90 minutes. Fibrinolysis if primary PCI not available within 120 minutes, followed by routine early angiography (2-24 hours). Radial access preferred. DES for all patients. Complete revascularization before discharge
- **ACC/AHA 2021 Chest Pain Guidelines**: High-sensitivity troponin for rapid rule-in/rule-out. 0/1-hour or 0/2-hour algorithm for rapid triage. Clinical decision pathways for chest pain evaluation
- **ESC 2023 Cardiogenic Shock**: Culprit-only PCI in AMI-CS. Hemodynamic monitoring with PA catheter. SCAI shock classification for standardized assessment

## India Practice Lens

ACS represents a major health burden in India with distinct challenges compared to developed nations:

- **Late presentation**: Indian STEMI patients present significantly later than in Western countries (median symptom-to-door time often exceeds 4-6 hours). Lack of awareness, transportation barriers, and financial concerns contribute to delays
- **Fibrinolysis still common**: Despite primary PCI being the preferred strategy, fibrinolysis remains the most common reperfusion therapy in India due to limited cath lab availability in rural and semi-urban areas. Pharmacoinvasive strategy (fibrinolysis + transfer for angiography) is a practical model being implemented through hub-and-spoke networks
- **STEMI networks**: Programs like the Tamil Nadu STEMI program, Kerala ACS registry, and STEMI India demonstrate successful implementation of regional STEMI treatment systems. Expansion to other states is ongoing
- **Young ACS patients**: A substantial proportion of ACS patients in India are under 45 years, necessitating aggressive secondary prevention and long-term management considerations
- **Antiplatelet considerations**: Ticagrelor and prasugrel availability and affordability have improved but clopidogrel remains the most commonly used P2Y12 inhibitor in India. Generic formulations of all three are available
- **Heparin use**: Unfractionated heparin is most commonly used; enoxaparin increasingly adopted. Bivalirudin and fondaparinux less commonly used due to cost
- **High thrombus burden**: Late-presenting STEMI patients often have high thrombus burden, contributing to no-reflow ([[no-reflow]]) and larger infarct sizes
- **Dual burden of care**: Many Indian ACS patients lack health insurance and bear out-of-pocket costs, influencing treatment decisions including stent choice, DAPT duration, and use of advanced therapies
- **Post-MI care**: Cardiac rehabilitation access is limited. Adherence to secondary prevention medications declines over time due to cost and awareness barriers. Polypill-based strategies are being studied for improving adherence in India
- **Research leadership**: Indian cardiologists contribute significantly to ACS research through registries (CREATE, Kerala ACS, STEMI India) and randomized trials relevant to resource-limited settings
- **Emergency medical services**: 108 and 112 ambulance services are expanding ACS access, but ECG transmission and prehospital management protocols remain inconsistent across states
- **Thrombolytic agent choices**: Streptokinase remains commonly used in India due to low cost (INR 2,000-3,000). Tenecteplase (INR 15,000-25,000) is preferred when available due to single-bolus administration and lower bleeding risk. Pharmacoinvasive strategy with tenecteplase is gaining traction
- **Door-to-balloon and symptom-to-door times**: Indian registries consistently report longer delays compared to Western benchmarks. Public awareness campaigns (recognizing chest pain as an emergency) and reducing financial barriers to seeking care are essential interventions
- **Regional disparities**: ACS outcomes vary dramatically between metropolitan centers (comparable to international standards) and rural/semi-urban settings (higher mortality). Addressing this gap through telemedicine, training, and infrastructure investment is a national priority
- **Post-discharge care models**: Nurse-led or community health worker-led follow-up programs can improve medication adherence and reduce readmissions. Mobile health reminders for medication compliance are being piloted across several Indian states
- **Women and ACS**: Indian women present later, receive less aggressive treatment, and have higher in-hospital mortality than men. Atypical presentations are more common. Gender-specific awareness campaigns and clinical pathways are needed
- **Quality metrics**: Indian institutions are increasingly tracking door-to-balloon time, STEMI mortality, and DAPT compliance as quality indicators to benchmark and improve ACS care delivery
