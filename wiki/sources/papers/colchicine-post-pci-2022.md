# Colchicine for Symptomatic Coronary Artery Disease After PCI

## Clinical Question
In patients with symptomatic coronary artery disease who have undergone percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), does the addition of colchicine to standard guideline-directed therapy reduce major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) compared to placebo?

## PICO

| Component      | Description                                                                                          |
|----------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Population** | Patients with symptomatic coronary artery disease who underwent PCI (6660 patients; mean age 60.9±10.6 years; 6 studies included patients with ≤13.5-day history of ACS) |
| **Intervention** | Colchicine (dosing regimens: 0.5 mg once daily to 0.6 mg twice daily) added to conventional guideline-directed therapy |
| **Comparator** | Placebo or no colchicine added to conventional guideline-directed therapy                           |
| **Outcome**    | Major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) including in-stent restenosis, repeat vessel revascularisation, stent thrombosis, stroke, resuscitated cardiac arrest, and all-cause death |

## Key Results
- **Primary Outcome - MACE**: Risk ratio 0.73 (95% CI 0.61-0.87); p=0.0003; NNT=41; minimal heterogeneity (I²=6%).
- **Event Rates**: Colchicine group 237/3347 (7.08%) vs control 303/3313 (9.15%).
- **Secondary Outcomes**:
  - Repeat vessel revascularisation: Risk ratio 0.47 (95% CI 0.31-0.72); p=0.0004; I²=0%.
  - Stent thrombosis: Risk ratio 0.50 (95% CI 0.25-0.98); p=0.05; I²=0%.
  - Stroke: Risk ratio 0.50 (95% CI 0.31-0.81); p=0.005; I²=0%.
  - In-stent restenosis: Risk ratio 0.64 (95% CI 0.36-1.15); p=0.14; I²=58% (not significant).
  - All-cause mortality: Risk ratio 1.12 (95% CI 0.49-2.58); p=0.79; I²=23% (not significant).

## What Changed?
- Colchicine showed a 27% relative risk reduction in MACE when added to standard therapy in post-PCI patients.
- Key benefits included reductions in repeat vessel revascularisation (53%), stroke (50%), and stent thrombosis (50%).
- Supports colchicine as a potentially low-cost adjunct for selected post-PCI patients, while dosing, safety, and implementation still require clinical judgment.
- Updated evidence, including the COPS trial, focuses specifically on PCI patients rather than just medical management.

## What Did Not Change?
- No significant reduction in all-cause mortality (RR 1.12; p=0.79).
- No overall reduction in angiographic in-stent restenosis, though a sub-analysis excluding an older balloon-angioplasty study showed a 53% reduction.
- Optimal dosing regimen remains unclear (0.5 mg once daily to 0.6 mg twice daily; periprocedural vs secondary prevention timing).
- Effect across stent types (bare-metal vs drug-eluting) not definitively established.
- Impact on periprocedural myocardial infarction not assessed.
- Safety concerns regarding non-cardiovascular death noted in other analyses, though not significant here.

## How I Would Explain This to a Patient
"After a heart attack or stent procedure, inflammation in your blood vessels can raise the risk of future issues like blockages, strokes, or stent clots. Colchicine, an old and inexpensive drug used for gout, helps reduce this inflammation. In a study of over 6,600 patients with stents, adding low-dose colchicine to standard medications like aspirin and statins lowered the chance of serious heart problems by 27%. For every 41 people treated, one major event was prevented. It mainly reduced the need for repeat procedures, strokes, and stent clots. However, it didn’t lower the risk of death, and we’re still figuring out the best dose and timing. It’s generally safe but can cause stomach upset or diarrhea in some."

## Relevance for Indian Practice
- **Cost**: Colchicine is generally low-cost in India compared to PCSK9 inhibitors or other secondary-prevention agents. A formal cost-effectiveness study in the Indian context has not been done as of this writing.
- **Use should follow current guideline indications.** Local prescribing rules vary; verify availability and pharmacy-class designation for your specific setting before relying on over-the-counter access.
- **Dosing uncertainty**: Optimal dosing regimen for this indication remains unclear (0.5 mg once daily vs 0.6 mg twice daily; periprocedural vs long-term).
- **Renal/hepatic safety**: Dose adjustment is needed in renal impairment, which is common in Indian diabetic patients; avoid in severe CKD.
- **Drug interactions**: Review for interactions (CYP3A4 inhibitors, P-gp inhibitors, statins) before initiation.
- **High-risk population**: The pooled study population (20–100% diabetic across the seven trials) overlaps with the Indian post-PCI population.
- **Bottom line**: Potentially low-cost adjunct in selected post-PCI patients, but use should follow current guideline indications, renal/hepatic safety checks, interaction review, and local prescribing rules. Not a replacement for guideline-directed antiplatelet, statin, or other secondary-prevention therapy.
