# Optical Coherence Tomography Imaging During PCI Impacts Physician Decision-Making: ILUMIEN I Study

## Clinical Question
Does intra-procedural optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging before and after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) alter physician decision-making and procedural strategy?

## PICO

| Component      | Description                                                                                                    |
|----------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Population** | 418 patients (467 stenoses) with stable angina (63%), unstable angina (22%), or NSTEMI (11%) undergoing elective or ad hoc PCI of de novo coronary stenoses |
| **Intervention** | Pre- and post-PCI OCT imaging with documentary FFR, with operators allowed to modify PCI strategy based on OCT findings |
| **Comparator** | Initial angiographically-guided PCI plan (pre-PCI) and angiographically-guided "best of care" result (post-PCI) |
| **Outcome**    | Change in PCI planning (stent length, diameter, number), post-PCI optimization rate, post-PCI FFR values, 30-day MACE |

## Key Results
- Pre-PCI OCT altered treatment planning in 55% of patients (57% of stenoses), with stent length changed (shorter in 25%, longer in 43%) and diameter adjusted (decreased in 31%, increased in 8%).
- Post-PCI OCT identified unsatisfactory abnormalities: malapposition (14.5%), under-expansion (7.6%), and edge dissection (2.7%).
- Further optimization based on post-PCI OCT occurred in 25% of patients (27% of stenoses), including additional in-stent post-dilatation in 81% and placement of 20 new stents (12%).
- Post-PCI FFR values were lower in cases with both pre- and post-PCI OCT reactions (0.86 ± 0.09 vs. 0.89 ± 0.07, P = 0.003), but improved to 0.90 ± 0.10 after optimization.
- Unsatisfactory findings decreased significantly post-optimization: malapposition from 48% to 9% (P < 0.001), under-expansion from 27% to 0% (P < 0.001), and edge dissection from 8% to 0% (P = 0.014).
- 30-day MACE rates were low: death (0.25%), MI (7.7%, ARC definition), repeat PCI (1.7%), and stent thrombosis (0.25%).
- Periprocedural MI was 0% in patients with both pre- and post-PCI OCT optimization, compared to 8.8–12.5% in other subgroups (P = 0.024).

## What Changed?
- OCT imaging significantly influenced physician decision-making, with 66% of patients experiencing pre- or post-PCI strategy changes.
- OCT detected abnormalities missed by angiography, such as edge dissection (27.6% by core lab), malapposition (32.1%), and under-expansion (41.3%) after "optimal" angiographic results.
- OCT-guided optimization corrected these issues and improved post-PCI FFR to comparable levels across subgroups.
- The study generated a hypothesis that OCT-guided optimization may reduce periprocedural MI, with 0% incidence in the fully optimized group.
- These findings led to the design of ILUMIEN III, a randomized trial comparing angiography, OCT, and IVUS-guided PCI optimization.

## What Did Not Change?
- As an observational, non-randomized study, clinical superiority of OCT-guided over angiography-guided PCI was not proven.
- No mortality benefit (death 0.25%) or reduction in stent thrombosis (0.25%) was demonstrated.
- Contrast use did not differ between optimization subgroups (P = 0.60).
- Long-term outcomes beyond 30 days were not reported (1-year data pending).
- No prescriptive OCT criteria were mandated, leading to wide variation in physician behavior (7–80% pre-PCI changes, 8–55% post-PCI changes per site).
- High-risk populations (STEMI, left main PCI, severe CKD, restenosis, stent thrombosis) were excluded.

## How I Would Explain This to a Patient
When we perform a procedure to open a blocked heart artery, we typically use X-ray dye pictures (angiography) to guide us. This study tested a special camera called OCT, which takes detailed images from inside the artery, to see if it helps doctors make better decisions. It found that OCT changed the plan in over half of the patients before placing a stent and led to additional improvements in one in four patients afterward. For instance, doctors adjusted stent size or used balloons to ensure the stent fit perfectly against the artery wall. While this might lower the chance of a heart attack during the procedure, we need larger studies to confirm if OCT is truly better than standard X-ray guidance.

## Relevance for Indian Practice
- **Cost Considerations**: OCT catheters add significant cost (₹50,000–80,000 per catheter), with increased resource use (additional stents in 4% of lesions) and longer procedure times (87.6 min without change vs. 106.4 min with full optimization, P = 0.004).
- **Availability**: OCT is limited to major Indian cardiac centers in metro cities, not smaller hospitals. This study included no Indian sites, with enrollment across the USA (36%), Europe (31%), and Japan (23%).
- **Follow-up Implications**: Low 30-day MACE suggests potential benefits in reducing periprocedural MI, which could be valuable for younger Indian patients with premature CAD.
- **Applicability**: Study demographics (37% diabetes, 72% hypertension) align with Indian CAD profiles, but exclusions of STEMI and complex lesions (left main, severe calcification) limit direct relevance.
- **Training Needs**: Wide variation in physician response to OCT (7–80% per site) underscores the need for standardized training and criteria before widespread adoption in India.

## Related Trials
- ILUMIEN III: A prospective randomized trial comparing angiography, OCT, and IVUS-guided PCI optimization, building on ILUMIEN I findings.
