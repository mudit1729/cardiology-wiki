# SELECT

## Clinical Question

In patients with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease who are overweight or obese but do NOT have diabetes, does semaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist) reduce major adverse cardiovascular events?

## PICO

| Component | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| **Population** | 17,604 patients aged >=45 with established ASCVD (prior MI, stroke, or symptomatic PAD) and BMI >=27 kg/m2, without diabetes |
| **Intervention** | Semaglutide 2.4 mg SC weekly |
| **Comparator** | Placebo injection weekly |
| **Outcome** | Primary: composite of CV death, non-fatal MI, or non-fatal stroke (3-point MACE) at event-driven analysis |

## Result

Semaglutide reduced the primary 3-point MACE endpoint by 20% (6.5% vs 8.0%, HR 0.80, 95% CI 0.72-0.90, p<0.001) at a mean follow-up of 39.8 months. All three components of MACE trended favorably: CV death (HR 0.85), non-fatal MI (HR 0.72), and non-fatal stroke (HR 0.93). Semaglutide produced a mean weight loss of 9.4% vs 0.9% with placebo. The benefit appeared consistent regardless of baseline BMI. Gastrointestinal adverse events were more common with semaglutide (nausea 20%, diarrhea 10%, vomiting 7%).

## What Changed?

SELECT was a landmark trial that established GLP-1 receptor agonists as a cardiovascular risk reduction therapy independent of diabetes management. For the first time, a weight-loss medication demonstrated MACE reduction in non-diabetic patients with established ASCVD. This expanded the therapeutic paradigm beyond traditional risk factors (LDL-C, blood pressure) to include obesity/metabolic health as a modifiable cardiovascular risk target. The trial positioned semaglutide not as a "diabetes drug" but as a "cardiovascular drug."

## What Did Not Change?

- The mechanism of benefit -- weight loss vs direct anti-inflammatory/anti-atherosclerotic effects -- remains debated.
- Long-term safety beyond 3-4 years is unknown.
- Cost and access remain major barriers worldwide.
- The trial did NOT include patients with BMI <27 or lean patients with ASCVD.
- It did NOT compare semaglutide to other weight-loss interventions (bariatric surgery, other GLP-1RAs).
- The optimal duration of therapy (lifelong vs time-limited) is unresolved.
- GI tolerability issues lead to significant discontinuation rates.

## How I Would Explain This to a Patient

"You had a heart attack (or stroke) and you are carrying extra weight. A large study tested whether a weekly injection of semaglutide -- a medication originally developed for diabetes but now known to cause significant weight loss -- could protect your heart even though you do not have diabetes. It found a 20% reduction in heart attacks, strokes, and heart-related deaths. Beyond weight loss, the medication appears to have direct heart-protective effects. The main side effects are nausea and stomach upset, which usually improve over time."

## Relevance for Indian Practice

- **Obesity epidemic**: India faces a growing epidemic of visceral obesity and metabolic syndrome. SELECT validates pharmacological intervention for CV risk reduction in this population.
- **Non-diabetic ASCVD**: Many Indian ASCVD patients have metabolic syndrome without frank diabetes. SELECT directly applies to this group.
- **Cost barrier**: Semaglutide 2.4 mg weekly costs approximately INR 5,000-8,000 per injection in India, making it prohibitively expensive for most patients.
- **Generic development**: Biosimilar GLP-1RAs are under development in India and could dramatically improve access.
- **Bariatric surgery alternative**: For Indian patients who cannot afford long-term semaglutide, bariatric/metabolic surgery may be a cost-effective one-time alternative for CV risk reduction.
- **Cultural considerations**: Weight stigma and reluctance to use "weight loss drugs" may affect acceptance in some Indian populations. Framing semaglutide as a "heart medication" may improve uptake.
