# FAME

## Clinical Question

In patients with multivessel coronary artery disease undergoing PCI, does FFR-guided PCI (treating only lesions with FFR <=0.80) reduce major adverse cardiac events compared to angiography-guided PCI (treating all angiographically significant lesions)?

## PICO

| Component | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| **Population** | 1,005 patients with multivessel CAD (stenosis >=50% in at least 2 of 3 epicardial vessels) referred for PCI with DES |
| **Intervention** | FFR-guided PCI: only lesions with FFR <=0.80 were stented |
| **Comparator** | Angiography-guided PCI: all angiographically significant lesions (>=50%) were stented |
| **Outcome** | Primary: composite of death, MI, or repeat revascularization at 1 year |

## Result

FFR-guided PCI significantly reduced the primary composite endpoint compared to angiography-guided PCI (13.2% vs 18.3%, p=0.02). FFR guidance resulted in fewer stents per patient (1.9 vs 2.7, p<0.001), less contrast use, and shorter procedure time. At 2-year follow-up, the benefit was maintained (8.4% vs 12.9% for death/MI, p=0.02). Approximately 35% of angiographically significant lesions had FFR >0.80 and were safely deferred. The study demonstrated that angiography alone overestimates the functional significance of many coronary lesions.

## What Changed?

FAME transformed interventional cardiology by establishing FFR as the standard for determining which coronary lesions warrant PCI. It showed that "less is more" -- treating only functionally significant lesions led to better outcomes with fewer stents. FFR became a Class I guideline recommendation for guiding PCI in multivessel disease. The trial fundamentally changed how interventional cardiologists think about lesion assessment, moving from a purely anatomic to a physiology-integrated approach.

## What Did Not Change?

- FAME was primarily a stable CAD trial; applicability to ACS was less clear (later addressed by other studies).
- The FFR threshold of 0.80 was adopted but remained somewhat arbitrary -- the "gray zone" (0.75-0.85) was not fully resolved.
- It did NOT compare FFR-guided PCI to medical therapy alone (that was FAME 2).
- FAME did NOT address the role of resting indices like iFR.
- The trial used first/early-second generation DES; modern stent technology may influence the risk-benefit calculation.

## How I Would Explain This to a Patient

"When we see blockages in your arteries on an angiogram, not all of them are actually causing problems with blood flow. We have a special pressure wire that can measure whether each blockage truly limits blood supply to your heart. Studies show that only treating the blockages that actually restrict blood flow -- rather than treating every blockage we see -- leads to fewer complications and better outcomes. It means you may need fewer stents, which is safer."

## Relevance for Indian Practice

- **FFR adoption**: FFR use has grown in India but remains far below optimal levels due to cost (INR 15,000-25,000 per wire), limited training, and time constraints.
- **Fewer stents**: In a market where stent overuse has been a concern, FFR provides an objective, evidence-based tool to guide appropriate stent use.
- **Cost paradox**: While FFR wires add procedural cost, the reduction in stent use and improved outcomes can save money overall.
- **Regulatory relevance**: The Indian government's stent price capping makes the incremental cost of FFR relatively more significant.
- **Training infrastructure**: Incorporating FFR into fellowship training is essential for broader adoption.
- **Alternative physiology**: When FFR is unavailable, quantitative flow ratio (QFR) from angiography may offer a cost-effective alternative.
