# FAME 2

## Clinical Question

In patients with stable coronary artery disease and at least one functionally significant stenosis (FFR <=0.80), does PCI plus optimal medical therapy reduce the composite of death, MI, or urgent revascularization compared to OMT alone?

## PICO

| Component | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| **Population** | 1,220 patients with stable CAD and at least one coronary stenosis with FFR <=0.80 (888 randomized; 332 with FFR >0.80 entered a registry) |
| **Intervention** | FFR-guided PCI with DES plus OMT |
| **Comparator** | OMT alone (with crossover to PCI permitted for refractory symptoms or ACS) |
| **Outcome** | Primary: composite of death, MI, or urgent revascularization at 2 years (initially; later extended) |

## Result

The trial was stopped early because of a highly significant reduction in the primary endpoint with PCI + OMT (4.3% vs 12.7%, HR 0.32, 95% CI 0.19-0.53, p<0.001). This was driven almost entirely by a dramatic reduction in urgent revascularization (1.6% vs 11.1%, p<0.001). There was no significant difference in death or MI at the initial follow-up. At 5-year follow-up, PCI + OMT continued to show lower rates of the composite endpoint, and a signal for reduction in spontaneous MI emerged. The registry of patients with FFR >0.80 treated with OMT alone had low event rates, confirming the safety of deferring PCI when FFR is negative.

## What Changed?

FAME 2 established that when a lesion is confirmed to be functionally significant by FFR, PCI provides a meaningful benefit over medical therapy alone, particularly in reducing the need for urgent revascularization. This reconciled the apparent contradiction between COURAGE/ISCHEMIA (PCI does not help in stable CAD) and clinical experience (PCI does help some patients) -- the key was selecting the right patients using FFR. It provided the strongest justification for FFR-guided PCI in stable disease.

## What Did Not Change?

- The early benefit was driven by "urgent revascularization," a softer endpoint; hard endpoints (death, MI) were not reduced initially.
- Early trial termination inflated the treatment effect estimate.
- It did NOT compare FFR-guided PCI to anatomy-guided PCI.
- The high crossover rate from OMT to PCI (~30% over 5 years) complicates long-term interpretation.
- It did NOT resolve whether all FFR-positive lesions need PCI or whether some can be safely managed with OMT and close follow-up.

## How I Would Explain This to a Patient

"We measured the blood flow through your blockage using a special pressure wire, and it shows the blockage is truly limiting blood supply to your heart. A study found that for blockages like yours, putting in a stent on top of medications reduces your chance of needing an emergency procedure in the future compared to medications alone. Medications alone are still a reasonable option, but the evidence suggests you will do better with both a stent and medications."

## Relevance for Indian Practice

- **Selective PCI justification**: FAME 2 provides the strongest argument for PCI in stable disease -- but only when FFR confirms functional significance. This is important in India where PCI appropriateness has been debated.
- **FFR as gatekeeper**: Using FFR before stable PCI can help defend clinical decisions against criticism and align practice with evidence.
- **Registry data**: The finding that FFR >0.80 patients do well with OMT alone supports deferring PCI in these patients, reducing unnecessary procedures.
- **Urgent revascularization context**: In India, urgent revascularization carries logistic challenges (access to cath labs, transfer delays). Preventing these events has additional practical value.
- **Cost-effectiveness**: FFR-guided PCI with fewer stents may be more cost-effective than ad hoc PCI of multiple lesions.
