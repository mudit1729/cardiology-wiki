# ISCHEMIA

## Clinical Question

In patients with stable coronary artery disease and moderate-to-severe ischemia on stress testing, does an initial invasive strategy (catheterization with revascularization by PCI or CABG) reduce major cardiovascular events compared to optimal medical therapy (OMT) alone?

## PICO

| Component | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| **Population** | 5,179 patients with stable CAD and moderate-to-severe ischemia on stress testing; excluded left main disease (>50%) on CTA |
| **Intervention** | Invasive strategy: cardiac catheterization followed by revascularization (PCI or CABG) plus OMT |
| **Comparator** | Conservative strategy: OMT alone with catheterization reserved for failure of medical therapy |
| **Outcome** | Primary: composite of CV death, MI, hospitalization for unstable angina, heart failure, or resuscitated cardiac arrest |

## Result

At a median follow-up of 3.2 years, there was no significant difference in the primary composite endpoint between the invasive and conservative groups (HR 0.93, 95% CI 0.80-1.08, p=0.34). The invasive strategy was associated with early procedural MI risk that was offset by fewer spontaneous MIs later. Quality of life and angina symptom burden improved more with the invasive strategy, particularly in patients with frequent angina at baseline.

## What Changed?

This trial fundamentally shifted the conversation around stable CAD management. It reinforced that revascularization in stable patients is primarily a symptomatic intervention, not a life-saving one. Clinicians became more comfortable pursuing aggressive medical therapy first, reserving catheterization for patients with refractory symptoms. Stress testing lost some of its perceived urgency as a triage tool toward the cath lab.

## What Did Not Change?

- The trial did NOT prove that revascularization is useless -- it clearly improved angina and quality of life.
- It did NOT address patients with left main disease (these were excluded by CTA screening).
- It did NOT show that stress testing itself is unnecessary -- ischemia severity still informs prognosis.
- It did NOT resolve whether very high-risk anatomic subsets (e.g., proximal LAD) benefit from revascularization.
- Long-term follow-up (beyond 5 years) data are still maturing and may change the calculus.

## How I Would Explain This to a Patient

"We did a large study of people like you who have blockages in the heart arteries and signs of reduced blood flow on a stress test, but who are otherwise stable -- meaning no heart attacks or emergencies. We found that doing a stent or bypass surgery right away did not prevent more heart attacks or deaths compared to just using the right medications. However, if you have chest pain that bothers you regularly, a procedure can help with those symptoms faster. So the decision really depends on how much your symptoms are affecting your life."

## Relevance for Indian Practice

- **Cost implications**: OMT is significantly cheaper than PCI or CABG. In resource-limited settings, this trial supports deferring invasive procedures when symptoms are manageable.
- **Access**: Many patients in India present without prior CTA to exclude left main disease. Physicians must remember that ISCHEMIA excluded left main stenosis, so this trial does not apply to those patients.
- **Follow-up adherence**: The benefit of OMT depends on medication adherence and regular follow-up, which can be challenging in rural Indian settings.
- **Generic medications**: High-quality generic statins, beta-blockers, and antiplatelets are widely available in India, making OMT a practical first-line approach.
- **Stress testing infrastructure**: Nuclear stress testing is not uniformly available; exercise treadmill testing remains the primary modality in many centers.
