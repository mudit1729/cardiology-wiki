# ORBITA

## Clinical Question

Does PCI improve exercise time compared to a sham procedure in patients with stable angina and severe single-vessel coronary stenosis already receiving optimized medical therapy?

## PICO

| Component | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| **Population** | 200 patients with angina, single-vessel coronary stenosis (>=70%), and preserved LV function, on optimized medical therapy for 6 weeks |
| **Intervention** | PCI with drug-eluting stent |
| **Comparator** | Sham procedure (catheterization with no intervention, patient blinded) |
| **Outcome** | Primary: increment in exercise time at 6 weeks post-procedure |

## Result

PCI did not significantly improve exercise time compared to the sham procedure (difference 16.6 seconds, 95% CI -8.9 to 42.0, p=0.20). There was no significant difference in peak oxygen consumption (VO2 max), angina frequency by Seattle Angina Questionnaire, or freedom from angina. However, PCI did improve dobutamine stress echo wall motion score, confirming a physiological effect of the intervention on myocardial ischemia.

## What Changed?

ORBITA was the first sham-controlled trial of PCI and exposed the magnitude of the placebo effect in interventional cardiology. It raised uncomfortable questions about how much of the symptomatic benefit attributed to PCI over decades was driven by patient expectation rather than true physiological improvement. The trial forced the field to adopt a higher evidentiary bar for claiming symptomatic benefit from elective PCI.

## What Did Not Change?

- The trial was small (200 patients) and powered only for exercise time, not clinical events.
- It did NOT study multivessel disease, left main disease, or ACS.
- Six-week follow-up was short; longer-term benefits may have been missed.
- The pre-randomization OMT optimization phase may have improved symptoms before PCI could add benefit.
- The trial did NOT disprove that PCI can relieve angina -- it showed the effect size is smaller than expected when placebo is accounted for.

## How I Would Explain This to a Patient

"Researchers did something unusual in this study -- they had everyone go through a heart catheterization procedure, but only half actually got a stent. Neither the patients nor their regular doctors knew who got the real treatment. After six weeks, both groups felt about the same in terms of exercise ability. This tells us that some of the benefit people feel after getting a stent may come from the belief that they have been fixed, rather than from the stent itself. It does not mean stents never help, but it suggests we should be cautious about expecting dramatic improvement."

## Relevance for Indian Practice

- **Placebo effect awareness**: Indian patients often report dramatic improvement after PCI, which may partly reflect placebo. This trial encourages objective assessment of symptom change.
- **Shared decision-making**: In a culture where doctor recommendations are rarely questioned, ORBITA supports taking time to discuss realistic expectations with patients.
- **Resource allocation**: If symptomatic benefit of PCI is smaller than assumed, the cost-benefit calculation for elective PCI shifts further toward medical therapy in resource-limited settings.
- **Study design inspiration**: ORBITA demonstrates the feasibility of sham-controlled procedural trials, a design that could be adopted in Indian clinical research settings.
- **Limitations in applicability**: Most Indian patients with stable CAD have multivessel disease and more advanced presentations; single-vessel disease is less representative of the typical patient.
