# ILUMIEN IV

## Clinical Question

Does OCT-guided PCI reduce target vessel failure compared to angiography-guided PCI in patients with complex coronary lesions or high-risk clinical features?

## PICO

| Component | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| **Population** | 2,487 patients with diabetes mellitus and/or complex coronary lesions undergoing PCI with everolimus-eluting stents |
| **Intervention** | OCT-guided PCI with specific optimization criteria (minimum stent area, edge dissection, malapposition assessment) |
| **Comparator** | Angiography-guided PCI |
| **Outcome** | Primary: target vessel failure (cardiac death, target vessel MI, or ischemia-driven TVR) at 2 years |

## Result

OCT-guided PCI achieved a larger minimum stent area compared to angiography-guided PCI (5.72 mm2 vs 5.36 mm2, p<0.001), confirming superior procedural results. However, the primary clinical endpoint of TVF at 2 years was NOT significantly different (7.4% vs 8.2%, adjusted HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.67-1.19, p=0.45). The trial was powered for a 40% relative reduction in TVF, which was likely too ambitious. Notably, major procedural complications were significantly lower in the OCT group, driven by fewer periprocedural MIs.

## What Changed?

ILUMIEN IV showed that OCT guidance reliably produces better stent deployment (larger stent area, fewer complications) but failed to demonstrate a statistically significant clinical benefit at 2 years. This nuanced result led to debate: the procedural advantages are real, but converting them into clinical endpoint differences may require larger trials or longer follow-up. The trial supported OCT as a tool for procedural optimization but did not make OCT guidance a Class I recommendation for all PCI.

## What Did Not Change?

- OCT guidance did NOT achieve a statistically significant reduction in TVF.
- The trial did NOT compare OCT to IVUS (both vs angiography, not head-to-head).
- The ambitious power assumption (40% relative reduction) may have set an unrealistic bar for a modern DES trial with low event rates.
- It did NOT address specific lesion subsets where OCT may have greater benefit (e.g., stent thrombosis evaluation, calcium modification).
- The role of OCT for calcium assessment and modification planning was not systematically studied.

## How I Would Explain This to a Patient

"We tested whether using a special light-based camera inside your artery during stent placement (called OCT) would lead to better outcomes than using standard X-ray guidance. The camera did help us place stents more precisely, with larger openings and fewer immediate complications. However, over two years, there was not a clear difference in major heart problems between the two approaches. We still recommend OCT for complex cases where the extra precision matters most."

## Relevance for Indian Practice

- **OCT availability**: OCT is less widely available than IVUS in India and is concentrated in academic and high-volume centers.
- **Cost considerations**: OCT catheters cost INR 25,000-40,000. Without a definitive clinical endpoint benefit, justifying routine use is challenging in cost-sensitive Indian settings.
- **Selective use**: The procedural benefits support OCT use in specific scenarios: stent thrombosis workup, calcium assessment, and complex bifurcation PCI.
- **Training gap**: OCT interpretation requires specific training that is not yet standardized in Indian interventional cardiology fellowship programs.
- **Subgroup analysis**: Indian patients with diabetes (a high-risk subgroup in ILUMIEN IV) may derive particular benefit, warranting subgroup-specific analysis.
