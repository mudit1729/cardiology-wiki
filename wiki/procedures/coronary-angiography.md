# Coronary Angiography

Coronary angiography is the gold-standard invasive diagnostic procedure for visualizing the coronary arteries using fluoroscopy and iodinated contrast injection. It remains the definitive method for assessing coronary anatomy, stenosis severity, and planning revascularization strategies.

## Indications

- Acute coronary syndromes (STEMI, NSTEMI, unstable angina)
- Stable angina refractory to medical therapy or with high-risk non-invasive test findings
- New-onset heart failure with suspected ischemic etiology
- Pre-operative evaluation before valvular or structural heart surgery
- Cardiac arrest survivors with suspected coronary etiology
- Inconclusive or discordant non-invasive testing

## Contraindications

- Active systemic infection or bacteremia
- Severe coagulopathy (uncorrected INR > 2.0)
- Decompensated renal failure without dialysis plan (relative)
- Severe contrast allergy without adequate premedication
- Patient refusal or inability to provide informed consent
- Acute stroke within 30 days (relative)

## Pre-Procedure Planning

- Review prior non-invasive imaging (stress test, CCTA, echo)
- Assess renal function (eGFR), electrolytes, CBC, coagulation profile
- Evaluate access site (Allen test for radial, femoral pulse assessment)
- Hydration protocol for CKD patients (normal saline 1 mL/kg/hr for 12 hours pre/post)
- Hold metformin on the day of procedure; manage anticoagulation bridging as needed
- Informed consent including discussion of risks (~1% major complication rate)

## Key Anatomy

- Left main coronary artery (LMCA) bifurcating into LAD and LCx
- LAD segments: proximal, mid, distal; diagonal branches (D1, D2)
- LCx segments: proximal, distal; obtuse marginal branches (OM1, OM2)
- Right coronary artery (RCA): proximal, mid, distal; PDA and PLV branches
- Coronary dominance: right dominant (85%), left dominant (8%), co-dominant (7%)

## Equipment

- Diagnostic catheters: Judkins Left (JL 3.5-5.0), Judkins Right (JR 4.0), multipurpose, Amplatz
- Guidewires: 0.035" J-tip wire for catheter exchanges
- Contrast: iodinated low-osmolar or iso-osmolar agents (iopamidol, iodixanol)
- Sheath: 5Fr or 6Fr arterial sheath (radial or femoral)
- Pressure transducer and manifold system

## Technique

1. Obtain arterial access (radial preferred, femoral as alternative)
2. Administer heparin (typically 2000-3000 units for diagnostic cases)
3. Advance J-tip wire to ascending aorta under fluoroscopy
4. Engage left main ostium with JL catheter; obtain standard views (RAO caudal, RAO cranial, LAO caudal, LAO cranial, AP cranial)
5. Exchange to JR catheter; engage RCA ostium; obtain standard views (LAO, RAO, AP)
6. Perform left ventriculography if needed (RAO 30 degrees)
7. Assess hemodynamics (LVEDP, aortic pressure)
8. Remove catheters, achieve hemostasis at access site

## Complications

- Access site bleeding or hematoma (1-5%)
- Coronary dissection or air embolism (<0.1%)
- Contrast-induced nephropathy (2-15% depending on baseline renal function)
- Stroke or TIA (<0.1%)
- Arrhythmias (transient bradycardia during RCA injection)
- Allergic reaction to contrast
- Death (<0.1% for diagnostic angiography)

## India-Specific Considerations

- Cost: INR 15,000-40,000 at government hospitals; INR 30,000-80,000 at private centers
- Radial access increasingly adopted but femoral still common in tier-2/3 cities
- Contrast volume minimization critical given high prevalence of diabetic nephropathy
- High prevalence of small-caliber radial arteries in Indian population
- Government schemes (Ayushman Bharat) cover diagnostic angiography at empaneled hospitals
- Iso-osmolar contrast agents may not be uniformly available; low-osmolar agents are standard
