# 2024 ACC/AHA Perioperative Cardiovascular Evaluation Guideline

## Top 5 Things That Changed

1. Integration of biomarkers: preoperative BNP/NT-proBNP and high-sensitivity troponin measurement recommended for risk stratification in patients with known or suspected CVD undergoing elevated-risk surgery.
2. Revised Cardiac Risk Index (RCRI) remains the recommended clinical risk calculator, but supplementation with biomarkers and functional capacity assessment provides more nuanced risk stratification.
3. Perioperative beta-blocker initiation abandoned: new beta-blocker starts are no longer recommended for perioperative cardioprotection, reflecting re-analysis of POISE and DECREASE trials. Continue existing beta-blockers.
4. Coronary revascularization rarely indicated solely for perioperative risk reduction: prophylactic PCI/CABG before non-cardiac surgery provides no benefit except in patients who would otherwise require revascularization independent of surgery.
5. Enhanced perioperative monitoring: continuous ECG monitoring and serial troponin measurement recommended for the first 48-72 hours post-surgery in high-risk patients, aiming to detect perioperative myocardial injury (PMI).

## Strong Recommendations

| Topic | Recommendation | Class | Level | Practical Note |
|-------|---------------|-------|-------|----------------|
| Stepwise risk assessment | Use RCRI or validated calculator for initial stratification | I | B | Functional capacity >=4 METs is reassuring regardless of risk factors |
| Continue chronic cardiac medications | Continue beta-blockers, statins, and antihypertensives perioperatively | I | B | Abrupt withdrawal increases perioperative risk |
| Preoperative ECG | 12-lead ECG for patients with known CVD or risk factors undergoing elevated-risk surgery | I | B | Compare to prior ECGs; new findings may alter management |
| Delay surgery after bare-metal stent | Wait minimum 30 days after BMS before elective non-cardiac surgery | I | B | Allows endothelialization of stent |
| Delay surgery after DES | Wait minimum 6 months after DES (ideally 12 months) | I | B | Discuss with interventional cardiologist if earlier surgery needed |
| Functional capacity assessment | Determine if patient can perform >=4 METs of activity | I | B | Climb flight of stairs, walk briskly, do heavy housework |
| Postoperative troponin monitoring | Measure troponin in high-risk patients for 48-72 hours | I | B | Perioperative MI often silent; early detection guides management |

## Controversial / Weak Recommendations

| Topic | Recommendation | Why Uncertain |
|-------|---------------|---------------|
| Preoperative stress testing | Consider only if results will change management | Routine stress testing delays surgery and rarely changes outcomes; reserve for patients with poor functional capacity and multiple risk factors |
| Preoperative coronary revascularization | Not recommended solely for perioperative risk reduction | CARP trial showed no benefit of prophylactic revascularization; treat based on standard indications |
| Perioperative aspirin | Continue in patients with coronary stents; otherwise hold 5-7 days before surgery | POISE-2 showed no benefit and increased bleeding with perioperative aspirin in non-stent patients |
| Bridging anticoagulation | Consider bridging only if high thrombotic risk (mechanical valve, recent VTE) | BRIDGE trial showed no benefit and increased bleeding with bridging in AF patients |
| Perioperative SGLT2i management | Hold 3-4 days before surgery to reduce ketoacidosis risk | Practice varies; some centers hold only 24-48 hours; balance against cardiorenal benefits |

## Drug Therapy Summary

- **Beta-blockers**: Continue if already taking; do not initiate for perioperative cardioprotection alone
- **Statins**: Continue perioperatively; perioperative initiation may have pleiotropic benefits but is not mandatory
- **ACEi/ARB**: Hold on morning of surgery to avoid intraoperative hypotension; restart when hemodynamically stable
- **Aspirin**: Continue if coronary stent within 6-12 months; otherwise hold 5-7 days pre-surgery; restart 24-48 hours post-surgery
- **P2Y12 inhibitors**: Hold clopidogrel 5 days, ticagrelor 5 days, prasugrel 7 days before surgery; restart as soon as bleeding risk permits
- **DOAC**: Hold based on renal function and bleeding risk (typically 24-48 hours for low-risk, 48-72 hours for high-risk surgery)
- **Warfarin**: Hold 5 days pre-surgery; bridge only if high thrombotic risk
- **Insulin**: Adjust dose; hold morning long-acting dose or reduce by 50%; monitor glucose closely
- **SGLT2 inhibitors**: Hold 3-4 days before major surgery

## India Practice Note

- **Drug availability**: All perioperative cardiac medications are available. High-sensitivity troponin assays are available at major hospitals but not universally accessible. BNP/NT-proBNP testing is available at urban tertiary centers but costs INR 1000-2000, which limits routine preoperative use.
- **Cost considerations**: Preoperative stress testing is often ordered reflexively in India, adding INR 3000-5000 per patient without improving outcomes. Judicious use of risk stratification tools can reduce unnecessary testing and costs. Preoperative echocardiography is frequently performed but should be reserved for patients with suspected cardiac disease.
- **Cath lab practicality**: Prophylactic PCI before non-cardiac surgery is still performed in India despite guideline recommendations against it. Surgeons and anesthesiologists often request cardiology clearance with the expectation of intervention. Education of the surgical team about perioperative risk evidence is important. Post-stent surgery timing discussions require close collaboration between cardiologist, surgeon, and anesthesiologist.
- **Follow-up adherence**: Perioperative myocardial injury (PMI) is significantly under-detected in India due to lack of routine troponin monitoring. Many hospitals do not have protocols for postoperative cardiac monitoring. Implementing a perioperative cardiac surveillance pathway with troponin measurements at 24 and 48 hours post-surgery would be a high-impact quality improvement initiative. Resumption of chronic cardiac medications post-surgery is often delayed or overlooked; discharge medication reconciliation is essential.
